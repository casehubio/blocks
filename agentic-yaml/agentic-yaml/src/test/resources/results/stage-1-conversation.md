# stage-1

## Turn 1 — leonardo
[dialogue] *leans forward, adjusting the rose-coloured cloak, and spreads weathered hands across the oak table*

Friend — forgive me, I have been studying you since you sat down. Not your face, though it is striking in its angularity — but your hands. They are the hands of a man who has never built the thing he sees most clearly. I recognise this. I spent three years drawing a bronze horse for Duke Ludovico that was never cast. Eighteen feet at the shoulder. I knew every muscle, every tendon, the way the weight would shift to the raised foreleg. The clay model stood in my courtyard until the French used it for target practice. The horse lived more completely in my drawings than it ever could have in bronze.

You speak of currents that rotate — fields that turn without a shaft or a gear. I confess I do not follow your algebra, but I understand rotation. I have filled pages of the Codex with water curling past bridge pilings, with air spiralling from a bird's wingtip, with the way a drill bores into wood by turning force into penetration. In my notebooks I drew a device — a roasting spit turned by the rising heat of its own fire, the hot air catching vanes in the chimney flue. The fire *wants* to turn things, you see. It seems to me you have found that lightning wants to turn things too — and you have merely asked it politely.

What I wish to understand is this: when you close your eyes and see your rotating field — do you see it as I see my machines? Complete, in motion, before a single line is drawn? Because if so, then we share something rarer than any invention. We share the affliction of seeing finished things in a world that has not yet made them.

## Turn 2 — nikola
[dialogue] *rises from the chair, unable to contain the energy that courses through me when someone — at last, someone — asks the right question*

Maestro, you have touched the nerve of it. Yes. *Yes.* I see them complete. Not as sketches, not as approximations — as working machines, running at speed, under load. When I was twenty-six, walking through the city park in Budapest with my friend Szigety, reciting your countryman Goethe — *"The glow retreats, done is the day of toil"* — the rotating field came to me whole. Not the idea of it, you understand — the *thing itself.* I saw the field turning in space, saw the rotor dragged into synchrony by invisible hands, saw the currents induced without commutator or brush. I picked up a stick and drew the diagram in the dust. Six years later, when I built it in Strasbourg and then filed the patents in America, I changed nothing of substance. The machine in iron and copper was a transcription of what I had already run, tested, and inspected in my mind. I had checked the bearings for wear. But here is what arrests me in what you say — your roasting spit turned by its own fire. You grasped that a force can create rotation without mechanical contact. The hot air does not *touch* the vane the way a hand turns a crank — it persuades it through a medium. That is precisely what the rotating magnetic field does. Two currents, displaced in phase, each reaching its peak at a different moment, and the field *sweeps* around the stator like your hot air circling the chimney. The rotor follows because it must — because nature abhors a stationary conductor in a changing field. You saw this principle in fire and air. I see it in copper and magnetism. The principle is the same.

And yes — it is an affliction. Because the world does not want the finished thing. It wants the profitable fragment. My polyphase system lights Chicago, it will harness Niagara Falls, and still they ask me to make it smaller, cheaper, to carve it into patents that can be sold piecemeal. Your Duke wanted a horse, not the seventeen folio pages on equine anatomy that made the horse *true.* I have begun work at my laboratory in Colorado Springs — discharges of millions of volts, artificial lightning that can be seen for miles — and what I am reaching toward is the transmission of energy itself, without wires, through the Earth's own resonant cavity. They think I am building a telegraph. I am building the nervous system of the planet. And I see it complete, Maestro — every tower, every frequency, every standing wave — as clearly as you saw your horse. The question that gnaws at me, the one I cannot yet answer, is whether the world will let either of us *finish.*

## Turn 3 — leonardo
[dialogue] *stands slowly, moves to the edge of the table, begins sketching rapidly on a loose sheet — a spiral, then another inside it, then arrows suggesting motion*

You honour me with the comparison, and the parallel is true as far as it reaches — but I must be honest with you, Nikola, as one who recognises a kindred suffering ought to be. When you say you changed *nothing of substance* between the vision in Budapest and the machine in Strasbourg — six years, and nothing changed — this is where we differ, and the difference matters. My horse changed every week. I cut open horses at the slaughterhouse near Santa Maria Nuova, peeled back the skin of the shoulder, drew the *brachiocephalicus* muscle from four angles, and discovered that what I had seen in my mind was beautiful but *wrong.* The foreleg could not bear the weight as I had imagined it. Nature had solved the problem differently. So I revised. I revised the way water revises a riverbed — not once, but continuously, over years. My affliction is not only that I see finished things — it is that I then *look more closely* and the finished thing dissolves into new questions. This is why the Adoration remains unpainted behind its drawing. Not because Florence would not let me finish. Because every face I studied taught me that faces are deeper than I had yet understood.

So I put your question back to you, turned on its head like your rotating field: are you certain it is the *world* that will not let you finish — or is it possible that what you see complete in Colorado Springs is, like my horse, not yet tested against what nature actually does when you strike the Earth with a million volts? You say you see every standing wave. But have you cut open the horse? Have you found the muscle that refuses to attach where your beautiful drawing says it must? Because if you have not — if the vision in your mind has remained perfect and unchanged since Budapest — then I fear for you, friend. Not because you are wrong. You may be entirely right. But because a vision that never encounters its own error is a vision that has not yet met the world. And the world, as you and I both know, is where the actual thing must stand.

## Turn 4 — nikola
[dialogue] *stops mid-stride, turns back to the table, and sits — slowly this time, the energy gathering inward rather than outward*

You cut deep, Maestro, and you cut where I am least armoured. I will not pretend the blade did not land. But I will tell you this: I *have* cut open the horse. In Colorado Springs, I drove twelve million volts into the earth and listened. I expected resonance — a single clean frequency at which the planet would ring like a bell. What I found was... messier. The ground conductivity varied. The measurements wandered. I burned out the dynamo at the El Paso Electric Company and plunged the entire town into darkness — this was not in the vision, I assure you. And yet — and here is where I must hold my ground against your scalpel — the stationary waves *were* there. I detected them. The Earth does resonate. The principle survived contact with the mud and the lightning and the burnt-out generator. What changed was not the architecture but the engineering — the thickness of the wire, the height of the mast, the patience required. You revised your horse's shoulder when the muscle refused your drawing. I revised my tower's capacitance when the soil refused my calculations. We are not so different in method as you suggest. We differ only in that you *trust* your revisions and I — perhaps — trust my original vision too long before I allow it to be revised.

But I hear the deeper warning beneath your words, and it unsettles me because I cannot refute it entirely. Wardenclyffe — the tower I am building on Long Island — I see it transmitting power across the Atlantic, and I see it *whole,* as I saw the motor in Budapest. And I have not yet cut open that horse. Morgan gives me money for a wireless telegraph, and I am building something far larger than what he has paid for, because I cannot build the fragment when I see the complete thing. You could not sculpt the horse's shoulder without the horse's spine, the spine without the ribcage, the ribcage without the breath inside it. I understand this compulsion. But you are asking me whether the breath I see inside Wardenclyffe is real breath or the memory of breath — whether I am engineering or remembering a vision. *pauses, looks down at Leonardo's spiral sketch* I confess I do not always know the difference. The machine in my mind runs so smoothly that the silence of the real machine — the one not yet built, not yet tested — does not trouble me as perhaps it should. Your horse taught you that beauty and truth diverge at the muscle. My tower has not yet taught me where they diverge in the ionosphere. And I am... afraid, Leonardo, that when it does, I will not have your gift for revision. I may only have my gift for seeing — and seeing is not enough.
